## 1-mark questions

### Design for safety

**Define safety in engineering.**
The practice of designing systems, processes and products so that risk to human life, property and the environment is minimised during both normal operation and failure conditions.

**What is Design for Safety?**
A planned, disciplined and systematic approach applied across the entire life cycle of a system to prevent or reduce accidents, not a check performed at the end.

**When and where did Design for Safety originate?**
In the 1950s, through the US Air Force, after it concluded that trial-and-error aircraft design was not viable. It produced MIL-STD-882.

**What is REX?**
Return of Experience, the practice of learning from deficiencies in previous designs so they do not recur.

**What is fail-safe design?**
A system that defaults to a safe condition on malfunction. Train brakes engage automatically if the signal is lost.

**What is inherent safety?**
Designing a system to be safe without relying on additional protective layers, for example by using less toxic materials.

**What is a safety factor?**
Designing components for higher load capacity than expected, usually 1.5 to 4 times the estimated load.

**Name the three safety analysis tools.**
FMEA, FTA (Fault Tree Analysis), HAZOP (Hazard and Operability Study).

**Expand FMEA and FMECA.**
Failure Mode and Effects Analysis. Failure Modes, Effects and Criticality Analysis.

**Who developed FMEA and when?**
The US military, in the 1940s.

**Differentiate DFMEA and PFMEA.**
DFMEA is applied during design to prevent failures. PFMEA is applied to process control during manufacturing.

**State the RPN formula.**
RPN = Severity × Occurrence × Detection, each rated 1 to 10.

**What is the simpler alternative to full FMEA?**
The risk matrix, where Rating = Probability × Severity, each rated 1 to 3.

**Name any three regulatory bodies in engineering safety.**
ISO, OSHA, IEC. (Also ASME, BIS, NFPA, CDSCO.)

**Which ISO standard covers occupational health and safety?**
ISO 45001. ISO 31000 covers risk management.

**What does the NSPE code require?**
Engineers must hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public.

**One-line cause of the Challenger disaster.**
A faulty O-ring failed in cold weather after management pressed to launch despite engineers' safety concerns.

**One-line cause of the Hyatt Regency collapse.**
A design change to the hanger rod connections doubled the load on a connector and was never re-verified. 114 people died.

### Design for reliability

**Define reliability.**
The probability that a product or system performs its intended function without failure for a specified period under stated conditions.

**Define MTTF with an example.**
Mean Time To Failure, for non-repairable items. If four bulbs last 900, 1000, 1100 and 1000 hours, MTTF = 4000 ÷ 4 = 1000 hours.

**Define MTBF.**
Mean Time Between Failures, for repairable items. It predicts the average time between system failures. Example: an ECU with a target of 100,000 hours.

**What do MTTR, λ and R(t) stand for?**
Mean Time To Repair, failure rate, and the reliability function.

**What is derating?**
Operating a component below its rated stress limit.

**What is robust design?**
Taguchi's approach of minimising the effect of variations, such as voltage fluctuation, on performance.

**Expand ALT, HALT and ESS.**
Accelerated Life Testing, Highly Accelerated Life Testing, Environmental Stress Screening.

**Give the key principle of reliability engineering.**
Reliability is designed in, not tested in. The design phase decides it; testing only confirms it.

The ability of a product, process or service to consistently meet or exceed customer requirements and expectations.

**State Crosby's and Juran's definitions.**
Crosby: conformance to requirements or specifications. Juran: fitness for use.

**Name Garvin's five categories of quality definition.**
Transcendent, product-based, manufacturing-based, user-based, value-based.

**List any three of Garvin's eight dimensions.**
Performance, Reliability, Serviceability. (Also Features, Conformance, Durability, Aesthetics, Perceived quality.)

**Differentiate QC and QA in one line.**
QC is reactive and finds defects by inspection. QA is proactive and prevents defects by improving the process.

**What is the most widely recognised QMS standard?**
ISO 9001.

**Expand TQM and name any three principles.**
Total Quality Management. Customer focus, continuous improvement (Kaizen), employee involvement.

**When and by whom was Six Sigma initiated?**
Motorola, in 1987.

**What defect rate does Six Sigma target?**
3.4 defects per million opportunities.

**Expand DMAIC and DMADV.**
Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control. Define, Measure, Analyse, Design, Verify.

**When do you use DMADV instead of DMAIC?**
DMAIC improves an existing process. DMADV designs a new process or product.

### Productivity

**Differentiate production and productivity.**
Production is the quantity of output produced. Productivity is the ratio of output to the inputs used.

**Define efficiency and effectiveness.**
Efficiency = actual output ÷ standard output, showing how well inputs are used. Effectiveness is the degree to which objectives are accomplished.

**Define total-factor productivity.**
Net output ÷ (labour + capital), where net output = total output minus purchased intermediate goods and services.

**Why must monetary values be deflated?**
To convert them to real values of a base year using the price index, so productivity can be compared across years.

**State the service productivity formula.**
Revenue from the service ÷ cost of producing the service. Globally, total revenue ÷ total cost.

**Why is service productivity only a partial measure?**
Because it is seldom possible to define one unit of a service.

**List the seven wastes in Lean.**
Overproduction, waiting, transport, extra processing, inventory, motion, defects.

**What is JIT?**
Just-In-Time: receiving goods only as they are needed in production, reducing inventory cost.

**What is work study?**
Method study (how tasks are done) combined with work measurement (time required), to optimise workflows.

### Quality and productivity tools

**List any three of the Seven Basic Quality Tools.**
Pareto chart, control chart, fishbone diagram. (Also check sheet, histogram, scatter diagram, flowchart.)

**State the Pareto principle.**
Vilfredo Pareto's observation that about 80% of effects come from 20% of causes.

**Name the six categories in a fishbone diagram.**
Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Environment.

**What are the components of a control chart?**
A central line for the mean, plus upper and lower control limits.

**Name two types of control chart.**
X̄ and R charts for variables data; P and NP charts for attribute data.

**Expand 5S.**
Seiri (Sort), Seiton (Set in order), Seiso (Shine), Seiketsu (Standardise), Shitsuke (Sustain).

**What is Value Stream Mapping?**
Visualising the entire production process, including material and information flows plus lead and cycle time, to identify waste.

**Name the three types of benchmarking.**
Internal, competitive, functional.

**What is Poka-Yoke? Give an example.**
Error-proofing through fail-safe mechanisms. Example: a plug that fits only one way.

**Expand SPC and name its capability indices.**
Statistical Process Control. Cp and Cpk.

**What is Kaizen?**
Continuous improvement through small incremental changes, central to Japanese manufacturing at firms such as Toyota.

**Name the two pillars of the Toyota Production System.**
Just-In-Time and Jidoka, automation with a human touch, meaning production stops when a defect is found.

**Name any three barriers to implementing quality initiatives.**
Resistance to change, lack of management commitment, inadequate training. (Also short-term focus.)

### CURA

**Expand CURA.**
Complexity, Uncertainty, Risk, Ambiguity.

**Name the three types of complexity.**
Structural (many parts or stakeholders), dynamic (conditions evolve over time), socio-technical (human and machine interaction).

**Distinguish aleatory and epistemic uncertainty.**
Aleatory arises from inherent randomness, such as weather or material properties. Epistemic arises from lack of data or limited models.

**State the risk formula.**
Risk = Probability of failure × Impact of failure.

**How does risk differ from uncertainty?**
Risk has known probabilities. Uncertainty may not.

**Name the four types of risk.**
Operational, safety, financial, legal or compliance.

**Name the four steps of the risk management process.**
Risk identification, risk assessment, risk mitigation, risk monitoring.

**State the five ISO 31000 steps.**
Identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, monitor and review.

**Give two sources of ambiguity.**
Poorly defined problem statements and conflicting stakeholder requirements.

**What is scenario planning?**
Developing multiple future scenarios (best case, worst case, expected) so teams can respond flexibly to uncertainty.

**What is Monte Carlo simulation used for?**
Random sampling and statistical modelling to estimate the range of possible cost and time outcomes in large projects.

**What went wrong with the Mars Climate Orbiter?**
Lockheed Martin used imperial units while NASA expected metric. The orbiter disintegrated, a $125 million loss.

**One-line cause of the Fukushima disaster.**
The plant was designed for an earthquake but not a tsunami of that scale, because data on extreme tsunami probability was insufficient.

**What was the 737 MAX problem?**
The MCAS software was not adequately documented or communicated to pilots, and new software was integrated into an older airframe.

## 5-mark questions

**Q1. Define safety in engineering design and explain the key considerations in design for safety.**

Safety is the condition of being protected from harm. In engineering it means designing so that risk to human life, property and the environment is minimised in both normal operation and failure conditions. Design for Safety is a planned, disciplined and systematic approach applied across the entire life cycle, originating with the US Air Force in the 1950s and formalised in MIL-STD-882.

Key considerations:
1. **Risk assessment** — identify hazards and evaluate consequences
2. **User training and education** — clear manuals for correct use
3. **Ergonomics** — comfortable designs that reduce musculoskeletal strain
4. **Redundancy and fail-safes** — safe operation continues if a primary system fails
5. **Accessibility** — usable by people of all abilities
6. **Material selection** — safe for users and environment, no harmful release in fire
7. **Human-machine interface** — intuitive, with safeguards against user error
8. **Product lifecycle** — safety through manufacturing, use and disposal

Supporting concepts are fail-safe design, inherent safety, safety factors of 1.5 to 4, and a formal safety lifecycle. Close with the NSPE requirement to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public.

**Q2. State and explain the three basic principles of design for safety.**

1. **Safety begins in the early stages** of design, development, testing and review. Hazard identification processes must be established early, because the sooner weaknesses are analysed, the easier, faster and cheaper the change is to implement.
2. **Learn from the deficiencies of previous designs.** Without applying lessons learned (REX), a design never advances, since it always starts from the same point.
3. **Apply engineering and management techniques together.** A safety programme must be planned and implemented so that safety analyses are integrated with the other factors influencing management decisions.

*Example:* the Hyatt Regency collapse of 1981 violated principles 1 and 3. A change to the hanger rod connections doubled the load on a connector, and poor design review let it through. 114 people died.

**Q3. Explain FMEA, its steps and the Risk Priority Number.**

FMEA is a systematic, step-by-step approach to identify and prioritise possible failures in a design, process, product or service, developed by the US military in the 1940s. A *failure mode* is the way something might fail; *effects analysis* studies the consequences.

Main steps: build a cross-functional team, set the scope, identify the functions of that scope, identify potential failure modes for each function (the most important activity), identify the effects, rate severity, and determine root causes.

Key elements are failure mode, effect, cause, Severity, Occurrence and Detection.

**RPN = S × O × D**, each rated 1 to 10. Example: S = 9, O = 3, D = 2 gives 54. Actions are prioritised by highest RPN, which is why a moderate-severity failure that is frequent and hard to detect can outrank a severe but rare one.

*Benefits:* proactive risk reduction, better reliability, early cost saving, documented knowledge, compliance with ISO 9001 and IATF 16949.

*Limitations:* a one-size-fits-all format, no ROI assessment, and lack of data. The simpler alternative is the risk matrix using Probability × Severity.

**Q4. Discuss a real-world engineering failure and the safety lapses that contributed to it.**

Take the **Challenger disaster of 1986**. A faulty O-ring in the right solid rocket booster failed in cold weather, and the shuttle broke up 73 seconds after launch.

*Root cause:* management pressure to launch despite safety concerns raised by engineers.

*Lapses:* reliability testing had not covered all operational conditions, particularly low temperature; technical concerns were not escalated effectively; commercial and schedule pressure overrode engineering judgment.

*Lessons:* engineers must speak up and document risks; testing must cover all operating conditions; communication between technical and managerial teams is vital.

Alternatives if you prefer: **Chernobyl (1986)**, where poor design, inadequate operator training and missing safety systems caused an explosion, leading to stricter IAEA controls. Or the **Hyatt Regency collapse (1981)**, where an unverified design change doubled a connector load.

**Q5. Compare quality and reliability. Can a system be reliable but unsafe, or safe but unreliable?**

| Aspect | Quality | Reliability |
|---|---|---|
| Timeframe | At the time of purchase or use | Over a period of time |
| Measure | Meets specifications and expectations | Performs consistently without failure |
| Focus | Initial features and performance | Long-term dependability |
| Example | A sharp new LED TV | A washing machine that works for 10 years |

Yes to both. A car that never breaks down but has no airbags is **reliable but unsafe**. A train that halts every time the signal drops is **safe but unreliable**: it never crashes, but it fails to deliver service. A reliable but unsafe system is unacceptable, and a safe but unreliable one is costly and inefficient, so a well-rounded design must integrate both through design reviews, simulation, testing protocols and user training.

**Q6. Explain the design techniques and strategies used to enhance reliability.**

Reliability is the probability of performing the intended function without failure for a specified period under stated conditions. Designing for it improves customer satisfaction, cuts warranty cost, protects brand reputation, lowers lifecycle cost and ensures compliance.

Six techniques:
1. **Redundancy** — duplicate components, such as dual aircraft engines
2. **Derating** — operating below rated stress limits
3. **Environmental protection** — shielding from the operating environment
4. **Stress-strength analysis** — comparing applied stress with material strength
5. **Modular design** — replaceable, independently testable modules
6. **Robust design (Taguchi)** — minimising the effect of variation

Additional strategies are predictive maintenance and statistical testing using MTBF, MTTR and availability. Test methods include ALT, HALT, burn-in and ESS.

*Case:* smartphone batteries were overheating due to charging circuit failure. Improved thermal pad placement and component derating raised MTBF by 45%.

Key line: reliability is designed in, not tested in.

**Q7. Explain the key reliability metrics with examples.**

- **MTTF (Mean Time To Failure)** — for non-repairable items, the average life before failure. If four bulbs last 900, 1000, 1100 and 1000 hours, MTTF = 1000 hours.
- **MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)** — for repairable items, the average operating time between failures. An automotive ECU may be designed to a 100,000 hour target.
- **MTTR (Mean Time To Repair)** — the average time to restore a failed repairable item.
- **λ (failure rate)** — failures per unit time; the inverse of MTBF for a constant rate.
- **R(t) (reliability function)** — the probability of surviving to time t.
- **Availability** = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR).

These support reliability across the life cycle: requirements at the concept stage, FMEA and fault-tolerant design during design, process capability during manufacturing, and maintenance strategy during operation.

**Q8. Explain Garvin's eight dimensions of product quality with suitable examples.**

Garvin identified eight dimensions along which quality is judged. Using one product throughout keeps the answer coherent:

| # | Dimension | Meaning | Smartphone example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Performance | Primary operating characteristics | Processor speed, battery backup |
| 2 | Features | Secondary functions that add appeal | Face unlock, NFC, wireless charging |
| 3 | Reliability | Functions without failure for a period | No crashes or faults for 2–3 years |
| 4 | Conformance | Meets design and operating specifications | Dimensions within tolerance, BIS certified |
| 5 | Durability | Use sustained before deterioration | Survives drops; battery lasts ~800 cycles |
| 6 | Serviceability | Ease of maintenance, repair, upgrade | Easy screen or battery replacement |
| 7 | Aesthetics | Look, feel and finish, subjective | Slim body, premium materials |
| 8 | Perceived quality | Impression from brand and reputation | An iPhone seen as premium due to branding |

Add the distinction examiners look for: reliability concerns failure *within* a period, while durability concerns total life *before* wearing out.

**Q9. Explain the classical definitions of quality and Garvin's five categories.**

Quality is the ability of a product, process or service to consistently meet or exceed customer requirements. In engineering it covers durability, performance, reliability, safety and aesthetic value.

- **Crosby:** conformance to requirements or specifications.
- **Juran:** fitness for use.
- **Garvin:** quality has five categories of definition.

| Category | In one line |
|---|---|
| Transcendent | Cannot be defined, but you know it when you see it |
| Product-based | The product has more of some desired attribute than others do |
| Manufacturing-based | Conforming to design and specification, having no defects |
| User-based | Fitness for use, satisfying consumer wants |
| Value-based | The best combination of price and features at acceptable cost |

The categories differ in viewpoint: manufacturing-based looks inward at the process, while user-based and value-based look outward at the customer.

**Q10. Differentiate quality control and quality assurance, and discuss the advantages and limitations of TQM.**

| | Quality Control | Quality Assurance |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Finds defects by inspection and testing during or after production | Prevents defects by improving processes from the outset |
| Stance | Reactive | Proactive |
| Timing | After the product exists | Before and during production |

**TQM** is a holistic approach in which all members of an organisation participate in improving processes, products, services and culture. Its principles are customer focus, Kaizen, employee involvement, process-oriented thinking, fact-based decisions and an integrated system. It shifts focus from inspecting finished goods to improving the whole value creation process.

*Advantages:* fewer defects, higher customer satisfaction, better employee engagement, lower long-term cost.

*Limitations:* results take time; it depends heavily on management commitment; it faces resistance to change, inadequate training, and overemphasis on short-term goals.

**Q11. How do ISO 9001 and Six Sigma contribute to the success of engineering organisations?**

**ISO 9001** is the most widely recognised Quality Management System framework globally. It standardises practices, ensures compliance, improves documentation and traceability, and gives customers external assurance of consistent quality.

**Six Sigma**, initiated by Motorola in 1987, is a data-driven methodology that eliminates defects and minimises variability, targeting 3.4 defects per million opportunities. Its principles are customer focus, data-driven decisions, process focus, proactive management, collaboration and continuous improvement.

**DMAIC** improves existing processes: Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control.
**DMADV** designs new ones: Define, Measure, Analyse, Design, Verify.

Together they lower operational cost, raise process capability, improve customer satisfaction, and build a documented knowledge base. ISO 9001 sets the system; Six Sigma drives measurable improvement inside it.

**Q12. Explain production, productivity, efficiency and effectiveness with examples.**

**Production** is the activity of producing goods and services, measured as the quantity of output. **Productivity** is the efficient utilisation of resources, measured as output ÷ input.

*Example:* 10,000 calculators made by 50 people at 8 hours a day for 25 days. Production = 10,000 calculators. Productivity = 10,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1 calculator per person-hour. If 10 more workers produce 20% more, production rises but productivity stays at 1.

**Efficiency** = actual output ÷ standard output, showing how well inputs are used. An operator making 120 pieces an hour against a standard of 180 has 66.67% efficiency.

**Effectiveness** is the degree of accomplishment of objectives, showing how well outputs meet their goals. If 60% of those pieces cannot be sold and 20% of the sold ones malfunction, effectiveness is low even though efficiency was measurable.

A process can therefore be efficient and still ineffective. Effectiveness uses both qualitative and quantitative measures.

**Q13. Explain partial, total-factor and total productivity, and the trade-off between them.**

| Measure | Definition |
|---|---|
| Partial | Output ÷ one class of input (labour, material, capital, energy) |
| Total-factor | Net output ÷ (labour + capital), where net output = total output minus purchased goods and services |
| Total | Total output ÷ the sum of all inputs |

*Example.* Output 1000; labour 300, material 200, capital 300, energy 100, other 50.
Labour = 3.33, material = 5.00, capital = 3.33, energy = 10.00, other = 20.00, TFP = 350 ÷ 600 = 0.583, total = 1000 ÷ 950 = 1.053.

Monetary values must first be deflated to a base year using the price index.

*Trade-off.* A new machine raised output from 100 to 120 parts per hour. Labour productivity rose 20%, but machine cost rose from Rs 40 to Rs 60 per hour, so machine productivity fell 20% and the combined figure fell 17%. A gain in one partial measure does not imply a gain overall, so always check the combined figure.

**Q14. How can engineers use Lean principles to improve process efficiency?**

Lean manufacturing minimises waste (*muda*) while maximising value. The seven wastes are overproduction, waiting, transport, extra processing, inventory, motion and defects.

Its tools are 5S for workplace organisation, value stream mapping to expose non-value-adding steps, and Kanban for flow control.

Related techniques:
- **JIT** — receive goods only as needed, cutting inventory cost
- **Time and motion study** — remove inefficiencies and standardise tasks
- **Work study** — method study plus work measurement
- **Automation** — robotics, AI, CNC and IoT reduce manual effort and raise precision

*Case:* the Toyota Production System rests on JIT and Jidoka, backed by Kaizen and employee empowerment, giving reliable and cost-effective vehicles.

*Barriers:* resistance to change, weak management commitment, poor training, short-term focus.

**Q15. Explain the seven basic quality tools and how they influence engineering decisions.**

1. **Cause-and-effect (Ishikawa or fishbone) diagram** — finds root causes; the bones are Man, Machine, Method, Material, Measurement, Environment
2. **Check sheet** — collects and organises data in real time
3. **Control chart** — monitors variation over time using a mean line with upper and lower control limits; X̄ and R charts for variables, P and NP for attributes
4. **Histogram** — shows the frequency distribution of data
5. **Pareto chart** — descending bars with a cumulative line, prioritising the vital 20% of causes behind 80% of effects
6. **Scatter diagram** — displays correlation between two variables
7. **Flowchart** — visualises the steps of a process

*Influence on decisions:* Pareto analysis tells engineers which 20% of defective components cause 80% of warranty claims, so corrective effort goes where it pays. Control charts detect drift in CNC precision before parts go out of tolerance. Together they enable data-informed decisions rather than guesswork. The right tool depends on the problem, the nature of the process and the goals of the organisation.

**Q16. Explain any five quality and productivity tools beyond the basic seven.**

- **5S** — Sort, Set in order, Shine, Standardise, Sustain. Creates a clean, organised workplace and cuts time wasted searching for tools.
- **Value Stream Mapping** — visualises material and information flows with lead and cycle time, exposing non-value-adding steps.
- **Benchmarking** — comparing processes against industry leaders. Internal, competitive or functional.
- **Poka-Yoke** — error-proofing through fail-safe mechanisms, such as a plug that fits only one way or a fixture that holds a part only when correctly oriented.
- **SPC** — statistical process control using control charts and the capability indices Cp and Cpk to keep output consistent.
- **Kaizen** — small continuous improvements driven by employees, central to Toyota and Mitsubishi.

**Q17. Discuss why understanding CURA is essential for modern engineers.**

Real engineering problems are rarely well-defined. They involve many variables, conflicting objectives, incomplete data and external constraints.

*The four factors:*
- **Complexity** — many interconnected parts where a change in one affects others unpredictably; structural, dynamic or socio-technical
- **Uncertainty** — incomplete knowledge, aleatory (randomness) or epistemic (lack of data). Unlike risk, it may have no known probabilities
- **Risk** — probability × consequence; operational, safety, financial or legal
- **Ambiguity** — information open to multiple interpretations, or unclear goals, as with a client asking for "high performance at low cost"

*Why it is essential:* complexity understanding reveals system interdependencies; uncertainty understanding prepares for variability; risk understanding focuses attention on probabilities and consequences; ambiguity understanding forces clear communication and problem framing.

*Cost of ignoring it:* project delays, system failures, budget overruns, safety incidents and legal liabilities. Managing it well produces innovation, adaptability and resilience.

*Case:* Fukushima. The plant withstood the earthquake but not the tsunami, because engineers had insufficient data on extreme tsunami probability. Risk models must account for low-probability, high-impact events.

Conclusion: mastering CURA transforms an engineer from a technician into a strategist.

**Q18. Explain the four CURA factors and how they are interrelated.**

Definitions as above, then the chain:

- **Complexity increases uncertainty.** More interacting parts means more you cannot predict.
- **Uncertainty creates risk.** What you cannot predict may go wrong.
- **Ambiguity makes assessment and communication difficult.** Unclear framing corrupts both the uncertainty estimate and the risk judgment.

Engineers must balance all four rather than treating them separately.

*Worked example, smart manufacturing (Industry 4.0):*
- Complexity: sensors, robotics, cloud and AI must integrate across hardware and software
- Uncertainty: how new technology scales, IoT data quality, rapid obsolescence
- Risk: cyberattacks, downtime from integration failure, heavy investment in unproven technology
- Ambiguity: no clear interoperability standards, and stakeholders interpret "smart manufacturing" differently

**Q19. What tools and methods are available to quantify and manage risk in engineering projects?**

- **Systems thinking** — viewing the problem holistically, understanding feedback loops and emergent behaviour
- **Scenario planning** — best case, worst case and expected case, so teams respond flexibly
- **ISO 31000 risk process** — identify, analyse, evaluate, treat, monitor and review
- **Design thinking** — human-centred, navigating ambiguity through stakeholder engagement and iteration
- **Monte Carlo simulation** — random sampling to estimate the range of cost and time outcomes
- **Redundancy and safety margins** — fail-safe mechanisms and buffers in high-risk systems
- **FMEA and the risk matrix** — quantifying risk as S × O × D, or as probability × severity

Quantification also uses **Risk = Probability × Consequence** directly, ranking risks so mitigation effort goes to the highest-scoring ones.

*Best practices:* early risk identification, cross-functional teams, prototyping under real conditions, standards to reduce ambiguity, and transparent documentation.

**Q20. How should engineers respond to an ambiguous or ill-defined problem?**

Ambiguity arises when information is open to multiple interpretations or when goals and priorities are unclear. Its sources are poorly defined problem statements, conflicting stakeholder requirements, and lack of common terminology or standards. It is most common in early design and in client communication using vague terms like "high performance".

Response strategies:
- **Improve communication and documentation** so terms mean the same thing to everyone
- **Engage stakeholders early and frequently** to surface conflicting requirements
- **Use prototyping and iterative design** so a physical artefact replaces a vague description
- **Develop decision-support tools** to make trade-offs explicit
- **Apply design thinking**, a human-centred approach that navigates ambiguity by iterating with users
- **Adopt standards** so interoperability and terminology are not left open

*Case:* the Mars Climate Orbiter was lost because one team used imperial units and the other metric. Clear standards and communication protocols are essential in multi-team projects.

**Q21. Give an example of a complex system and explain how interdependencies increase uncertainty.**

Take **urban transportation planning**, such as a metro system.

- **Complexity:** roads, rail, signals, pedestrian paths, vehicles and human behaviour interact. Stakeholders include government, citizens, operators and environmental groups. Subsystems such as stations, power, signalling and rolling stock are interconnected at huge scale.
- **How this increases uncertainty:** because subsystems are coupled, a change in one propagates unpredictably. Population growth affects demand, which affects rolling stock needs, which affects power draw and station design. Fuel prices and mobility trends such as EVs and shared mobility shift the demand model again. Data gaps in traffic surveys compound this.
- **Resulting risk:** cost overruns and delays, safety failures, political risk if the system disappoints the public.
- **Ambiguity:** conflicting goals between low cost and environmental sustainability, and unclear policy direction between metro rail and road expansion.

The general principle is that complexity increases uncertainty, uncertainty creates risk, and ambiguity makes both harder to assess.

**Q22. Discuss the role of ethics when engineers must make decisions with incomplete information.**

Engineers are not just technical experts but decision-makers who must act before all facts are available.

Their responsibilities:
- **Identify and clarify** stakeholder expectations rather than assuming them
- **Communicate risks and uncertainties transparently**, including what is not known
- **Use data and modelling tools** to support judgment instead of intuition alone
- **Build resilient systems** that tolerate being wrong, through redundancy and safety margins
- **Balance trade-offs** honestly between cost and safety, performance and reliability

The professional standard, from the NSPE code, is to hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public. When information is incomplete, this means erring toward caution, documenting the uncertainty, and escalating concerns rather than proceeding silently.

*Case:* the Challenger engineers knew the O-ring behaviour in cold was untested. The ethical failure was not the incomplete data but the decision to proceed despite it, and the failure to insist the concern was heard.
